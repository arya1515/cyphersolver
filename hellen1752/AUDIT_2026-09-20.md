## Why the old statistics need qualification

`parse.py` deletes every question mark before recognizing numbers. For example,
`2 1 ? ?` becomes the definite number 21. It also processes empty newline fragments
as unreadable groups, treats the untagged heading year 1752 as ciphertext, and
discards cipher preceding a cleartext tag on the same line. Underlines and carets
are discarded entirely. These are parsing defects, not manuscript evidence.

`audit_transcription.py` keeps the original segment, source line, page, uncertain
characters and marks. It strips tagged cleartext without discarding adjacent cipher.
Its numeric projection accepts only unambiguous 1–4 digit strings after removing
marks, explicitly as a projection rather than a claim that marks are meaningless.
Missing separators, doubtful digits and oversized numbers remain unresolved;
apparently legible numbers still require manuscript checking.

The six 1763 records have 1,234 unambiguous numeric tokens, 634 different values.
These are segment counts, not final counts of cryptographic symbols: an unresolved
segment may contain two numbers where a separator is missing. No source transcription
was overwritten, and the old `ct*.txt` files remain for reproducibility, not as reliable inputs.
Regression checks confirm retention of cipher before a cleartext tag, preservation of
uncertainty and underlines, and exclusion of empty trailing line fragments.

Visual inspection of `IMG_R1953_I13448_P3.png` confirms that the near-repeated
three-line block beginning 1426 825 963 occurs twice on the manuscript itself.
The earlier suggestion that this was a DECODE transcription duplication is wrong.
The two copies are not identical; do not delete either or assume which is correct.
This session did not collate all their digits.

The previous low density correlations reject particular frequency-rank mappings.
They do not prove a random two-part code, prove grouped homophones, or establish
that ciphertext-only recovery is impossible. Codebook structure and homophones
remain hypotheses. No long coherent plaintext has been recovered under any model.

## Archival research

De Leeuw's *Cryptology and statecraft in the Dutch Republic* is available in full:
https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/3074957/12760_Thesis.pdf
Local research copy and extracted text are under `decode/de_leeuw_thesis.*`.
Its chapter reprints *The Black Chamber in the Dutch Republic and the Seven Years'
War, 1751–63*, *Diplomacy & Statecraft* 10(1), 1999, pp. 1–30.

Useful references from article notes 45–46, 72 and 76 (PDF pp. 112–114): Fagel
5177 holds British decipherments with Lyonet's additions; Boerhaave Museum,
Arch 162-r holds Lyonet/Croiset's account. De Leeuw reports no Prussian codes
broken April 1757–October 1763; the later Thulemeyer code began 14 October 1763.
This weakens the assumption that a Dutch decipherment of code C necessarily survives.
It does not settle whether the particular 1752 or 1756 target has a decipherment.
