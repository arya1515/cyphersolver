Ireland / England · nomenclator · 1634–35 · alphabet recovered

Lord Maltravers to the Earl of Ormonde

Dorking, 13 September 1634 and Whitehall, 22 January 1634/5 · HMC, Calendar of the Manuscripts of the Marquess of Ormonde, i (1895), pp. 28–29

The letter alphabet, its nulls and the layout of the key recovered from 59 printed figures; the nomenclator read from context

Daniel Bourdeau · September 2026

On this pageThe documentsStructureThe keyReadingWhat remains uncertainMethodTry the keySources
Summary. Two letters from Henry Frederick Howard, Lord Maltravers, to James Butler, Earl (later Duke) of Ormonde, contain 59 figures of a numerical cipher, printed undeciphered by the Historical Manuscripts Commission in 1895 and listed as unsolved by S. Tomokiyo. The figures divide cleanly into letters (below 91), nulls (91–111) and a small nomenclator (112 and above). The doubled letters in letters and councellor are written with consecutive figures, which betrays a regular key: consonants in alphabetical order, three figures each from 7, vowels from 64. With it every spelled word reads — angry, letters, Crosby, a councellor — and the nomenclator falls into place from the history of the 1634 Irish Parliament: Kildare came to England "because he was angry [with] the Lord Deputy, [and the King] refused to see him because he brought no letters from the Lord Deputy"; and in January 1635 "the Lord Deputy hath written … much in praise of Ormonde … for his good carriage in Parliament, and upon his motion Ormonde is to be a councellor" — Ormonde was sworn of the Irish Privy Council that month. Status: the alphabet is solved; the nine nomenclator codes are inferred, not proved.

01 The documents

Maltravers (1608–1652), heir of the Earl of Arundel, sat in the Irish Parliament of 1634 and was a friend and correspondent of the young Earl of Ormonde. His letters from England report court news; the sensitive parts — the quarrel between the Earl of Kildare and the Lord Deputy, Wentworth, and what was being written about Ormonde himself — are in figures. The originals are in the Ormonde papers (now National Library of Ireland); the only witness online is the HMC calendar, whose figures were checked against the page scans.

02 Structure

  • Nulls, 91–111. Nine values (93 95 97 98 99 100 103 108 111); seventeen of their eighteen occurrences stand at the edge of a cipher run or beside a code number — 93 185 95, 99 149, 95 186 98, 98 218 97, 100 174 103, 111 95 221. Tomokiyo's guess is confirmed: they pad the names.
  • Nomenclator, 112 and above. 113 143 149 174 185 186 218 221 270, each standing alone; 270 is a code being assigned ("for whom 270 thus hereafter").
  • Letters, below 91. The spelled runs contain 49 50, 69 70, 28 29, 11 12 — doubled letters written with adjacent figures. Each letter therefore owns a block of consecutive figures, the commonest design of the period (Tomokiyo's survey of Wentworth-circle ciphers notes the same "three or six figures to each letter in regular arrangement").

03 The key

Three cribs fix the grid. "brought no 28 69 49 50 70 44 89" must be letters; "is to be 64 [null] 11 79 85 35 12 69 28 29 79 44" gives a councellor; "concerning 10 44 79 47 8 59, for whom 270 thus hereafter" — the man whose fortunes were about to "go worser" in January 1635 — is Crosby (Sir Piers Crosby, purged from the Irish Council by Wentworth that winter). All three fit one arrangement, and an exhaustive scan of 42 regular grids (start 1–11, block size 2–4, vowel blocks 2–6) ranks it first by English quadgram score:

Consonants: the nineteen of the 24-letter alphabet (i = j, u = v). Attested figures: b 8 · c 10 11 12 · g 21 · l 28 29 · m 31 · n 35 · r 44 · s 47 · t 49 50 · y 59 · a 64 65 · e 69 70 · o 79 · u 85. The vowel block sizes (five each from 64, or a = 3 and the rest 6) cannot be fixed from this text; 1–6 are unattested.

04 Reading

Red = spelled in cipher · blue = nomenclator code, value inferred from context · nulls dropped

13 September 1634. My lord of Kildare is long ago in England though I have not seen him; it is said he came over because he was angry [113 = with?] [186 = the Lord Deputy]; [143 = the King?] refused to see him because he brought no letters from [186 = the Lord Deputy].
22 January 1634/5. You desire to know what [185] hath written unto [149] concerning Crosby, for whom 270 thus hereafter; of which I can give you no account at all, only by what I hear it is like to go worser with him rather than better. [186 = the Lord Deputy] hath written unto [218] much in praise of [174 = Ormonde] in general, and particularly for his good carriage in [221 = Parliament], and upon his motion [174 = Ormonde] is to be a councellor, in which courses [174 = Ormonde] shall do well to continue.

The history confirms the reading. George FitzGerald, 16th Earl of Kildare, fell out with Wentworth over the Kildare estates during the 1634 Parliament and crossed to England that summer; Sir Piers Crosby, having opposed the Lord Deputy in the same Parliament, was removed from the Irish Council early in 1635; and Ormonde, who had backed Wentworth in the Commons debates, was sworn of the Irish Privy Council in January 1635 — the very month of the second letter. 186 as the Lord Deputy is used three times consistently; 174 as Ormonde ("your lordship") three times; 221 as Parliament follows "his good carriage in". The remaining codes (113, 143, 149, 185, 218) are persons or connectives that the text does not determine.

05 What remains uncertain

  • The fourth figure of angry is printed 26, which the grid makes k; r is 43–45. The scan confirms the print, so either Maltravers slipped or the 1895 transcriber misread 44/45. "Angry" is the only word that fits a n g ? y.
  • 89 at the end of letter is either u (a slip for s, if the vowel blocks are six wide) or a null/plural sign (if they are five wide).
  • 31 (= m) before 174 in "upon his motion 31 174 is to be" is unexplained — perhaps "m[y lord]" begun and abandoned.
  • The nomenclator values are contextual. A key sheet, if one survives, would be among the Ormonde papers (NLI) or the Arundel papers; neither is online.

06 Method

  1. OCR of the HMC volume from the Internet Archive; both letters extracted and every figure checked against the page images.
  2. Regex scan of the whole volume (and the New Series volumes) for other figure runs: the only other ciphers (1644, 1650s) use different keys; no key or decipherment is printed.
  3. Positional test of 91–111 (edge-of-run statistic) → nulls. Consecutive-figure doublets → block alphabet.
  4. Cribs from the clear frame and the events of 1634–35; exhaustive scan of regular grids scored by English quadgrams; the crib-derived grid wins.
  5. For comparison, a simulated-annealing homophonic solver on the 28 letter-figures alone returns quadgram-pleasing nonsense — at this length the structure, not the statistics, carries the solution.
