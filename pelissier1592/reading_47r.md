## Summary

Pelissier, the League's envoy at Philip II's court, is reporting what he said to the king. He argues that the French (the
nation meant is not certain; the key word decodes "harannois") have always done willingly what was asked of them rather than
under force, and that when forced they would rather die to a man. He cites what happened under "the last king" (Henri III):
people rose against him over exactly this. Mildness, friendship and benefits will lead them anywhere, so if Philip follows that
course he will win honour and reputation with every nation. The page then speaks of the ruin of the "wicked" (the royalists)
and of Philip's memory being made immortal. The king heard him out without interrupting. He said he would first put some
questions, starting with whether Pelissier spoke on Mayenne's orders ("par commandement"). Pelissier replied that he had had
no word from Monseigneur since the two armies faced each other near Caudebec (spring 1592). Instead, his friends and those
devoted to the cause kept him informed of popular feeling and the enemy's tricks. Since many reports agreed, he thought it his
duty to tell the king the plain truth. He asks that the grievances he raised be remedied: past failures feed enemy slander
and the people's loss of hope. He says Philip had spared no expense, but the illness was growing, so stronger remedies were
needed. People in the provinces know better than others what is good or bad; the sentence runs over onto f. 47v.
