## Summary

Pelissier, the League's envoy at Philip II's court, sets out for Jeannin how things are being argued in Spain. First comes the suspicion that the King (or his ministers) wants France kept in a balance of mutual weakness, so that he can dispose of the French as he likes and take his share of a divided crown where it suits him. People back this up with his past conduct, which he has let drag on "in a length that promises no end". Pelissier then gives the case made by those who would recognise Henri of Navarre. God has "called him as by miracle" to the succession ahead of so many others, so he must be recognised even though a heretic. Having lived "from his youth in perpetual pain and labour, poor and miserable", and now at the turn of his age, he will want peace and will try to keep his subjects. If he ever moved against the Catholics they would unite against him and be released from their oath of fidelity. Pelissier warns that, even though these arguments can be refuted, they work on war-weary people. These people fear ruin from continued war more than from the establishment of the "[315]" (probably the Roy), and they hear such ideas from relatives and friends of the same nation. He fears the Catholic princes could not stop a conclusion so harmful to religion in France and all Christendom. The deciphered close urges that His Majesty (Philip II), who alone can cure this malady, take up so pious an action. To his title of "Catholic" he would then add "most Christian" and "liberator of Christendom". The sentence runs on to f. 50r.
